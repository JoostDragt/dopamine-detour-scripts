## ✍️ STEP 2 — SCRIPT (VOICEOVER)

> Tightened and sharpened from the source script: shorter sentences, harder verbs, more direct address. Every line earns its place.

### [HOOK]

Quick — how many tabs do you have open right now?

In your browser… and in your head.

If that made you laugh, or wince, give me sixty seconds. Because what people call your "short attention span" is almost certainly *not* what's actually going on.

Your brain isn't broken. It's not lazy. It's not low on willpower.

It just takes a different route to the same place.

### [MAIN CONTENT]

Let's start with the name everyone gets wrong — including people who *have* it.

ADHD stands for Attention-Deficit / Hyperactivity Disorder. And honestly? That name lies to you.

Because it isn't a *deficit* of attention. People with ADHD can pay ferocious attention — sometimes too much, to the wrong thing, at the worst possible moment.

The truth is simpler and stranger: ADHD is a difference in how the brain *regulates* attention, energy, and impulse. The spotlight isn't dim. The hand holding it just has a mind of its own.

And here's what matters most — ADHD is *neurodevelopmental.* Big word, simple idea: it's a brain wired differently, from early on. You don't catch it. Screen time doesn't cause it. It's not a phase, and it's not a character flaw. It shows up in roughly five to seven percent of kids, and just under five percent of adults — in every country, every culture, on earth.

So… what about "ADD"?

You might be thinking, "Wait — wasn't there ADHD *and* ADD?" Great question. Here's the part almost nobody explains.

"ADD" — Attention Deficit Disorder, without the hyperactivity — was a real, official term… decades ago. Back in 1994, the experts retired it. Today it's *all* called ADHD. There is no separate diagnosis called ADD anymore.

So why won't the word die? Because it described something real — the quiet, dreamy, "lost in my own head" version that never looks hyper. That version didn't disappear. It just got a new name.

Because ADHD isn't one thing. It comes in three flavors of the same brain.

One — *inattentive.* The old "ADD." Daydreamy, easily sidetracked, loses the thread mid-sentence, starts things and drifts. Quiet on the outside. A hundred tabs on the inside.

Two — *hyperactive-impulsive.* Restless, always moving, talks fast, acts before thinking. This is the one everybody pictures — which is exactly why it gets spotted in some kids and missed in everyone else for years.

Three — *combined.* Both at once. And that's actually the most common one.

Same wiring. Different surface. So when someone says "you don't *look* like you have ADHD" — they're just picturing door number two.

Now the real question: *why* the detour?

A huge part of the answer sits right behind your forehead — the prefrontal cortex. Think of it as your brain's air-traffic control. It runs your executive functions: planning, starting, resisting distraction, tracking time, holding the goal in mind.

In ADHD, that tower runs differently. And it leans on two chemical messengers you'll hear about constantly on this channel — *dopamine* and *norepinephrine.*

Here's the myth-buster: dopamine isn't the "pleasure chemical." It's the brain's signal for *what's worth paying attention to* — motivation, interest, reward. And in the ADHD brain, that signal fires unevenly. Something interesting, urgent, or new? The signal spikes, and focus locks in like a laser. Something boring — important, but not urgent? The signal goes quiet. And the brain physically fights you to stay on it. Not *won't.* Can't — without a battle.

That's the detour. The road to "just do the thing" was never a straight highway. It winds. And that — right there — is where this channel gets its name. Understand the dopamine, and almost everything else about ADHD finally clicks.

### [ENDING]

So here's what I want you to walk away with.

A brain that chases novelty is also a brain that can hyperfocus for hours, link ideas nobody else links, stay calm when everything's on fire, and spot the thing the whole world drove straight past.

The same wiring that makes a boring form feel impossible can make a hard, interesting problem feel *electric.*

ADHD is real. It's medical. For a lot of people it makes life genuinely harder — and we're never going to pretend otherwise. But it is *not* a broken version of a normal brain.

It's a different route. Real challenges. Real strengths.

Next time, we crack open the engine itself — *why* this brain is always hunting the next spark, and how to work *with* it instead of against it.

And if any of this felt a little too familiar — like I was describing your own hundred open tabs — subscribe, and come along for the detour.

Because the road's not broken.

It's just yours.
