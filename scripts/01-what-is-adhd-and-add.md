**Channel:** Dopamine Detour

**Target length:** ~7–8 minutes (≈1,250–1,350 spoken words)
**Language:** English (international audience)

---

## Production notes

- **Voice (ElevenLabs):** warm, calm, curious — like a smart friend explaining, not a lecturer. Medium pace. Let the pauses (`—` and line breaks) breathe. This is the channel's first impression, so the tone sets the brand.
- **Pacing for distractible viewers:** a visual change roughly every 4–8 seconds. Never let one static image sit for more than ~10 seconds.
- **On-screen text:** keep keywords on screen (3–5 words max) to anchor attention. Marked as `[TEXT]`.
- **Recurring motif:** the *winding road* (the detour) vs. the *straight highway* (the neurotypical default). Re-use it in the hook and the ending so the episode feels "closed."
- **Accuracy:** vetted against current clinical consensus (DSM-5-TR). Key point: **"ADD" is an outdated term** — it's all called ADHD now, in three *presentations*. The script says this explicitly so we're correct from episode one.

### 0:45 — WHAT IT ACTUALLY IS

**[VO]**
Let's start with the name everyone gets wrong — including a lot of people who have it.

ADHD stands for *Attention-Deficit / Hyperactivity Disorder.* And honestly? That name is misleading.

**[VISUAL]** The words "Attention Deficit" appear, then a red line crosses out "Deficit."

**[VO]**
Because it isn't really a *deficit* of attention. People with ADHD can pay enormous amounts of attention — sometimes too much, to the wrong thing, at the wrong time.

The real story is that ADHD is a difference in how the brain *regulates* attention, energy, and impulse. It's not that the spotlight is dim. It's that the hand on the spotlight has a mind of its own.

**[VISUAL]** A theatre spotlight swinging around a dark stage on its own, lighting up random things — a clock, a snack, a phone — but skipping the script on the podium.

**[VO]**
And here's the part that matters: ADHD is what scientists call a *neurodevelopmental* condition. That's a long word for a simple idea — it's a brain that's wired differently, from early in development. You don't catch it. You don't develop it from too much screen time. It's not a character flaw or a phase.

**[TEXT]** Neurodevelopmental = wired differently.

**[VO]**
It's one of the most studied conditions in all of psychology — and it shows up in roughly five to seven percent of kids, and a bit under five percent of adults, all over the world, in every culture.

### 2:00 — SO WHAT ABOUT "ADD"?

**[VISUAL]** An old, dusty filing cabinet. A drawer labelled "ADD" slides open; the label is faded and yellowed.

**[VO]**
Now — you might be thinking, "Wait, I thought there was ADHD *and* ADD. What happened to ADD?"

Great question. And the answer clears up a lot of confusion.

**[VO]**
"ADD" — Attention Deficit Disorder, *without* the hyperactivity — was a real, official term... decades ago. But back in 1994, and again in the current diagnostic manual, the experts retired it.

**[VISUAL]** The "ADD" label peels off the drawer and a new, clean label slides on: "ADHD."

**[VO]**
Today, it's *all* called ADHD. There's no separate diagnosis called ADD anymore.

So why do people still say it? Because the old word described something real — the quiet, dreamy, "lost in my own head" version of ADHD that doesn't look hyper at all. That version didn't disappear. It just got a new home.

**[TEXT]** "ADD" → now a *type* of ADHD.

### 3:00 — THE THREE PRESENTATIONS

**[VISUAL]** Three doors side by side, each glowing a different color. The same silhouette of a person stands in front of all three — same brain, three "flavors."

**[VO]**
Because ADHD isn't one single thing. It comes in three presentations — three flavors of the same brain.

**[VISUAL]** Door one opens. Inside: someone staring out a window, daydreaming, surrounded by gently floating thought-bubbles.

**[VO]**
One — *predominantly inattentive.* This is the one people used to call "ADD." Daydreamy, easily sidetracked, loses the thread of a conversation, starts things and drifts off. Quiet on the outside, a hundred tabs on the inside.

**[VISUAL]** Door two opens. Inside: someone tapping a foot, fidgeting, bouncing with restless energy, words tumbling out fast.

**[VO]**
Two — *predominantly hyperactive-impulsive.* Restless, always moving, talking fast, acting before thinking. This is the version most people picture — and it's why ADHD gets spotted earlier in some kids and missed for years in others.

**[VISUAL]** Door three opens. Inside: a swirl that blends the daydream bubbles and the restless energy together.

**[VO]**
And three — *combined.* A mix of both. This is actually the most common one.

**[TEXT]** Inattentive · Hyperactive · Combined

**[VO]**
Same underlying wiring. Different way it shows up on the outside. So when someone says "you don't *look* like you have ADHD" — that's usually because they're only picturing door number two.

### 4:30 — WHY THE DETOUR? (the brain part)

**[VISUAL]** Zoom inside the brain. A glowing region at the front lights up — the prefrontal cortex — labelled simply.

**[VO]**
So what's actually different in there?

A big part of the story lives right behind your forehead — a region called the prefrontal cortex. Think of it as the brain's air-traffic control. It's in charge of the *executive functions*: planning, starting tasks, resisting distractions, managing time, and keeping your goals in mind.

**[VISUAL]** An air-traffic control tower directing planes (tasks). In the ADHD version, the controller is brilliant but the radio signal keeps cutting in and out — planes circle, some land perfectly, some get forgotten mid-air.

**[VO]**
In ADHD, that control tower works a little differently — and the messengers it relies on are two brain chemicals you'll hear about a lot on this channel: *dopamine* and *norepinephrine.*

**[TEXT]** Dopamine · Norepinephrine

**[VISUAL]** Two streams of glowing particles trying to cross a gap between brain cells. Some particles get reabsorbed too quickly and vanish before they land.

**[VO]**
Dopamine isn't the "pleasure chemical" people think it is. It's more like the brain's *signal for what's worth paying attention to* — motivation, interest, and reward. And in the ADHD brain, that signaling runs differently. Interesting, urgent, novel, or fun? The signal fires strong, and focus locks in like a laser. Boring, repetitive, or "important but not urgent"? The signal goes quiet — and the brain physically struggles to stay on task. Not won't. *Can't, without a fight.*

**[VO]**
That's the detour. The road to "just do the thing" isn't a straight highway. It winds. And that's exactly where this channel gets its name — because once you understand the dopamine, almost everything else about ADHD starts to make sense.

**[TEXT]** Interest runs the engine — not effort.

## Accuracy / fact-check notes (not spoken)

- **"ADD" is outdated:** The standalone term *Attention Deficit Disorder* was replaced in the DSM in 1994; the current DSM-5-TR uses **ADHD with three presentations** (Predominantly Inattentive, Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive, Combined). The script states this directly.
- **Prevalence figures** ("~5–7% of children, just under 5% of adults") reflect commonly cited international estimates. Safe, conservative framing — no exact stat is leaned on hard.
- **"Combined is the most common presentation"** — widely reported in clinical literature; kept as a general statement.
- **Neurotransmitters:** dopamine **and** norepinephrine are the two most implicated; the script names both rather than over-simplifying to "just dopamine." Dopamine is framed as a **motivation/salience signal**, not the pop-culture "pleasure chemical" — this is the accurate framing and also sets up the channel's recurring theme.
- **Prefrontal cortex / executive function** framing is mainstream and uncontroversial.
- **Not caused by screen time / parenting / sugar** — the "you don't catch it, it's not a phase" line pre-empts the most common myths (which Episode 4 covers in full).
- **Deliberately avoided:** medication claims, "cures," and any diagnostic advice. Tone stays educational, not clinical advice.
