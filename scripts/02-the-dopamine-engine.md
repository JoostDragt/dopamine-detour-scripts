# Episode 2 — The Dopamine Engine

**Channel:** Dopamine Detour
**Series phase:** 1 — Foundation (what & why)
**Target length:** ~8–9 minutes (≈1,300–1,400 spoken words)
**Language:** English (international audience)
**Format:** Faceless. AI visuals (Higgsfield) + AI voiceover (ElevenLabs).

---

## Production notes

- **Voice (ElevenLabs):** same warm, calm, curious "smart friend" tone as Episode 1 — keep the voice consistent across the series. Medium pace; let `—` and line breaks breathe.
- **Pacing for distractible viewers:** a visual change roughly every 4–8 seconds. Never let one static image sit longer than ~10 seconds.
- **Recurring motif:** the *engine / fuel* metaphor is the spine this time, but bookend with the **winding road vs. straight highway** so it ties back to Episode 1 and the channel name.
- **NEW — colorized doodle (Option 1):** Episode 2 evolves the look. Sam's black marker linework stays, but the world is now **in color** — see `02-PRODUCTION-PACKAGE.md`. Dopamine is always the **golden-amber** signature.
- **Accuracy:** dopamine framed as a *motivation / "wanting"* signal, not the pop "pleasure chemical." ADHD = lower dopamine availability + faster reuptake. Interest-based-nervous-system framing credited as a clinical *model*, not a hard rule.

## SCRIPT

### 0:00 — HOOK (first 20 seconds)

**[VISUAL]** Sam slumped at a desk. A single sheet of paper in front of him reads **"IMPORTANT TASK."** He stares at it. A beat. Then his hand drifts — almost on its own — toward a glowing golden phone just out of frame. Coral stress-squiggles flicker above his head.

**[VO]**
You sit down to do the one task you've been putting off all week.

It's important. You *know* it's important.

And yet… nothing happens. Your hand drifts toward your phone.

**[TEXT]** You're not lazy.

**[VISUAL]** The desk and clutter dissolve. Sam stands up. Behind him, a little doodled car engine flickers to life, glowing golden, then sputters and dies — *put-put-put… nothing.*

**[VO]**
You're not lazy. You're not broken. Your engine just runs on a different kind of fuel.

Today, we're looking under the hood — at the dopamine engine that drives the ADHD brain.

**[TEXT]** The Dopamine Engine

---

### 0:40 — THE ENGINE METAPHOR

**[VISUAL]** Cutaway of Sam: inside his chest sits a little hand-drawn car engine, a fuel line running up into his head. The engine glows warm amber when fuel flows.

**[VO]**
Imagine your motivation is an engine.

To move — to *start* a task, to keep going, to *care* — that engine needs fuel. And that fuel has a name: dopamine.

**[VISUAL]** The word **"PLEASURE"** appears next to the engine — then a marker line crosses it out and writes **"WANTING"** above it.

**[VO]**
Now, you've probably heard dopamine called the "pleasure chemical." That's not quite right.

Dopamine isn't the feeling of the reward. It's the feeling of *wanting.* It's the spark that makes you go and get the reward in the first place.

No spark — no movement. The engine just sits there.

**[TEXT]** Dopamine = the spark to *start*.

---

### 1:30 — WHAT DOPAMINE ACTUALLY DOES

**[VISUAL]** A neurotypical brain shown as two friendly neuron-blobs with a small gap between them. Golden dopamine coins hop **smoothly** across the gap, landing neatly. A little fuel gauge beside them sits steady at full.

**[VO]**
Every time you do something your brain decides is worth it — eat when you're hungry, finish a job, get a like — a little burst of dopamine fires.

It's your brain's way of saying: *"Yes. That. Do it again."* It tags certain actions as worth your energy — and it builds the motivation to *start* them.

**[VISUAL]** Pull back: a plain stick figure climbs calmly into a tiny car and drives off down a straight grey road, fuel gauge full, no drama.

**[VO]**
In a typical brain, this system is pretty steady. A boring-but-important task still gets *enough* fuel to get going. The reward of "it's finally done" is enough.

The engine turns over — and off they go.

**[TEXT]** Enough fuel → it just starts.

---

### 2:45 — THE ADHD ENGINE RUNS DIFFERENTLY

**[VISUAL]** Same two neuron-blobs — but now the golden coins leap toward the gap and little **vacuum pumps** (coral) yank most of them straight back before they can land. Only one or two make it across. The fuel gauge sits near empty.

**[VO]**
But in the ADHD brain, this engine runs differently. And two things are going on at once.

First — there's often *less* dopamine available to begin with. Fewer of the signals that say "this matters."

**[TEXT]** ① Less fuel to start with.

**[VO]**
And second — the brain clears that dopamine away too fast. Picture little pumps sucking the fuel back out almost the second it arrives.

**[TEXT]** ② Drains away too fast.

**[VISUAL]** Sam straining to push a stalled little car uphill, sweating coral drops, while a plain stick figure cruises effortlessly past him on the smooth straight road.

**[VO]**
So the spark fires… and fizzles. For ordinary, everyday tasks, the everyday amount of dopamine just isn't *enough* to turn the engine over.

It's not that you don't *want* to do it. It's that the fuel that's supposed to turn "wanting" into "doing" drains out before it can move you.

From the outside, it looks like laziness. On the inside? It's an engine flooding and stalling.

**[TEXT]** Not won't. *Can't — yet.*

---

### 4:15 — WHY THE BRAIN HUNTS FOR STIMULATION

**[VISUAL]** Sam in the center, surrounded by tempting objects that each radiate golden dopamine sparks — a phone, a snack, floating browser tabs, a TV. He reaches for the brightest one. (Colorized callback to the Episode 1 "100 tabs" energy.)

**[VO]**
So what does an under-fuelled engine do? It goes *hunting.*

If your brain can't get enough dopamine from the boring thing in front of you, it reaches for something — anything — that gives it a faster, bigger hit.

The phone. A snack. A new tab. A pointless argument online. Refreshing the same app for the tenth time.

**[VO]**
This is the ADHD brain *self-medicating* — trying to top up the tank just to feel normal.

**[TEXT]** Distraction = topping up the tank.

**[VISUAL]** Split panel. Left: a shiny **NEW** project box bursting with golden sparks, Sam thrilled. Right: the *same* box three weeks later — dull, greyed-out — Sam slumped beside it, fuel gauge empty.

**[VO]**
It's also why *novelty* feels so good, and routine feels so flat. A brand-new project? Huge burst of fuel. The exact same project three weeks later? Tank's empty again — and starting feels impossible.

**[TEXT]** New = fuel. Routine = empty.

---

### 5:45 — THE INTEREST-BASED NERVOUS SYSTEM

**[VISUAL]** A doodled dashboard with four big glowing amber buttons in a row: **NOVELTY · INTEREST · CHALLENGE · URGENCY.** Sam presses them and his engine roars to life, fuel gauge climbing. Off to the side, a grey button labelled **"because I should"** stays dark and unlit.

**[VO]**
Here's the part that changes everything.

The ADHD engine doesn't run on "importance." It runs on *interest.* Researchers describe it as an **interest-based nervous system** — and it roars to life for four things.

**Novelty** — something new. **Interest** — something you genuinely find fascinating. **Challenge** — something that pushes you. And **Urgency** — a deadline breathing down your neck.

**[TEXT]** Novelty · Interest · Challenge · Urgency

**[VISUAL]** Sam at 3 a.m. under a doodled moon, writing furiously, while a big glowing deadline-clock pumps golden fuel straight into him. Dark teal night, amber glow.

**[VO]**
Notice what's *not* on that list: "because I should."

That's why you can write the whole essay the night before — urgency finally floods the engine with fuel. It's why you'll happily learn *everything* about a new hobby, but can't answer one boring email.

It was never about willpower. It's about what switches the engine *on.*

**[TEXT]** Interest runs the engine — not effort.

---

### 7:00 — REFRAME + WORKING WITH IT

**[VISUAL]** Sam looks at his own engine thoughtfully, then starts *deliberately* feeding it golden sparks — turning a boring grey task into a little game board, sticking a clock on it, pulling up a chair so a friend sits beside him.

**[VO]**
Once you see it this way, you stop fighting your brain — and start working *with* the engine you've actually got.

You can build the fuel in on purpose. Turn a boring task into a game. Add a little urgency. Do it next to someone else. We'll dig into exactly how in a future video on the ADHD toolkit.

**[TEXT]** Next: build the fuel in on purpose.

**[VISUAL]** Sam's stalled car from earlier — now transformed, engine glowing golden — drives happily along the **winding yellow road**, curving toward a bright, colorful horizon and a scenic viewpoint that the straight grey highway drove right past.

**[VO]**
Because here's the truth: your engine isn't broken.

It just runs on a different fuel — novelty, interest, challenge, and the spark of something that genuinely matters to *you.*

Stop pouring in the wrong fuel, start giving it the right kind — and that same restless engine becomes one of the most powerful things about you.

**[TEXT]** Your engine isn't broken.

## Accuracy / fact-check notes (not spoken)

- **Dopamine = motivation / "wanting," not "pleasure":** The script frames dopamine as an *incentive-salience / anticipation* signal (the drive to act), which is the accurate framing and consistent with Episode 1.
- **ADHD dopamine differences:** Presented as (1) lower dopamine availability and (2) faster reuptake (dopamine-transporter activity clearing it too quickly). This is the mainstream model; kept general, no hard stats leaned on.
- **Stimulant medication** deliberately *not* discussed in detail (one neutral "self-medicating" line about everyday distraction only) — keeps the episode educational, not clinical advice. Save meds for a dedicated video.
- **Interest-based nervous system (Novelty / Interest / Challenge / Urgency):** drawn from Dr. William Dodson's clinical descriptions. Presented as a helpful *model* of motivation, not a diagnostic checklist.
- **"Not won't — can't" framing** mirrors Episode 1's language for series consistency and to pre-empt the "lazy / willpower" myth (covered fully in Episode 4).
- **Deliberately avoided:** dosage/medication claims, "cures," diagnostic advice.

> ⚠️ Description disclaimer (reuse): *"Educational content, not medical advice. If you think you have ADHD, talk to a qualified professional."*
